The anatomy of a card payment fee

Every card payment carries several charges, usually rolled into a single rate. Here is what each one is, who sets it and which ones can be negotiated.

12 October 20262 min readRumstar

In this guide

  1. The five components
  2. What to do with this

When a customer pays by card, your statement usually shows one number: a percentage, sometimes with a fixed amount per transaction. That number is a bundle. Understanding what is inside it is the first step to knowing whether you are paying the right price.

The five components

ChargeWho receives itWho sets itNegotiable?
InterchangeThe bank that issued the cardThe card schemes, within regulatory capsNo
Scheme feesVisa, Mastercard and other networksThe card schemesNo
Acquirer marginYour acquirer or payment providerYour contractYes
Gateway feesThe gateway providerYour contractYes
Other chargesUsually your providerYour contractMostly

Interchange

Interchange is paid by your acquirer to the customer's card issuer. It varies by card type, whether the card is consumer or commercial, whether the card was present, and where the card was issued. In the EEA it is capped for most consumer cards.

Scheme fees

The card schemes charge for using their networks. These fees appear as many separate items and change from time to time. They are not capped.

Acquirer margin

This is what your acquirer keeps for processing and settling your payments. It is the main subject of negotiation, and on blended pricing it is the part you cannot see.

Gateway fees

The gateway connects your checkout to the acquirer. Some providers bundle it into the rate, others charge per transaction or per month.

Other charges

Authorisation fees, refund fees, chargeback fees, PCI compliance fees, statement fees, currency conversion and minimum monthly charges. Individually small, together often significant.

What to do with this

Ask your provider for an itemised statement if you do not already receive one. Then separate the pass-through costs from the margin. That margin, plus the smaller charges, is what a negotiation can change.
